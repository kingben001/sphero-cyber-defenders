Learning goals

Three ideas to take away.

1 · Spot it

Phishing is a trick

A fake message tries to get someone to click, share information, or take an unsafe action.

2 · See it

A middle person intercepts

A person-in-the-middle gets between two devices or people communicating and may read or change the messages.

3 · Protect it

Pause and verify

Use trusted apps and networks, avoid sharing private information in response to unexpected requests, and ask a trusted adult or IT staff for help.

Your opening demonstration

Keep it playful and use pretend details.

You: “Has anyone heard of phishing or a man-in-the-middle attack? Any volunteers for a quick demo?”

If no students volunteer, use your colleague, who has agreed to play along: “There are eight people in here and I only got one volunteer? I know it’s early, but I need more than that. For a $20 Federal Reserve note, can I get someone to answer two questions?”

You: “Young man in the back with the orange glasses—come on up. What should I call you for this demo, and what is your email address?”

Colleague: “You can call me Alex, and my pretend email is alex@example.test—”

You, interrupting: “It doesn’t matter what your name is!” Then pause and reveal: “That was the wrong move. I used a reward to make a personal-information request feel okay, and I interrupted instead of checking what was actually needed. We used pretend details; no one should share a real email for a prize.”

Bridge to the lesson: This skit is a phishing/social-engineering-style trick. A person-in-the-middle attack is different: someone secretly gets between two communicating sides to read or change their messages. Phishing can sometimes help an attacker gain access, but the two terms do not mean the same thing.

Before students arrive

Prepare the tech so the 45-minute plan works.

Equipment and timing

Sphero’s lesson is “Person-in-the-Middle Attacks: Who’s There?” (code 65563). It is listed for grades 5–9, uses three BOLTs and three programming devices per group, and expects familiarity with IR communication and events. Its full version can take up to an hour, so this event uses selected steps and the starter program.

For eight students, make two groups of four; each group can assign three robot roles and one observer/timekeeper. If your earlier set of 30 BOLT+ robots is available, the lesson needs only six for these two groups before open play.

Two related ideas

Phishing and MITM are different.

Phishing

The lure

A deceptive message tries to trick a person into clicking or sharing information.

Person-in-the-middle

The interceptor

A third party gets between two sides communicating and may read or change messages.

A phish can sometimes help an attacker gain access, but phishing is not the same attack as MITM.

Presenter cue

Use “lure versus interceptor.” That short contrast keeps the concepts separate.

Materials and roles

Plan each team before class.

Per team

  • Three charged BOLT or BOLT+ robots
  • Three programming devices with the Sphero Edu lesson open

  • A clear floor or table space

Student roles

  • User: the device sending a request
  • Website: the service replying
  • Middle: the interceptor BOLT

Facilitator says

Make the model concrete.

“First, the user BOLT and website BOLT will pass messages directly. Then the middle BOLT will get between them. We’ll see the messages take a different path and, in the challenge, change what is delivered.”

“This is an isolated classroom model. We are not connecting to or testing a real website or network.”

Run the shortened Sphero lesson

Start from the official program.

1 · 3 min

Show the original connection

  1. Open the lesson’s linked program on all three devices.
  2. Assign user, website, and middle roles; leave the middle out for this first run.
  3. Set the user and website BOLTs about 12 inches apart.
  4. Run the website program, then the user program.
  5. Ask what the robots send back and forth.
2 · 5 min

Insert the middle BOLT

  1. Place a book or binder between the first two robots.
  2. Put the middle BOLT to the side with a clear line of sight to both.
  3. Start the website and middle programs first.
  4. Start the user program last.
  5. Observe the delay and explain who can now see the messages.
3 · 4 min

Try the message-switch challenge

  1. Use the official lesson’s step 5 and its starter-program comments.
  2. Switch the message channels as the lesson directs.
  3. Run the programs and test whether the middle BOLT changes what the user receives.

If a group is still setting up, let it observe a ready group instead of rushing through new blocks.

4 · 3 min

Debrief before free play

  • What was different from the original connection?
  • What could the middle BOLT see or change?
  • How was this different from the phishing skit?

Teacher answer key

1. A fake message meant to trick someone into clicking, opening, or sharing information.

2. A third party gets between communicating sides and may read or alter messages.

3. Phishing is the deceptive lure; MITM is the interception in the communication path. Safe actions include using a trusted app/address or network, not entering details after an unexpected prompt, and asking a trusted adult or IT staff.
